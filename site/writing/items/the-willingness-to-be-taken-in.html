The Willingness to Be Taken In

Created March 26, 2025 · Updated March 26, 2025 · 572 words

Criticism, both positive and negative, often reveals more about the critic than the work. This is something C.S. Lewis helped me see more clearly in his book, “An Experiment in Criticism”.

A shallow reader (viewer, listener, etc.) doesn’t just miss the point. They create a new point and then critique that. You think they’re telling you something about the book, the movie, the idea, but they’re really just telling you something about themselves. Criticism can reveal something true about the work too, but it necessarily reveals something about the critic.

Without humility, even an insightful critic becomes unreliable.

Lewis’ appeal is for us to receive and then evaluate.

This is why good criticism is rare. You have to risk being “taken in.” You have to give the work every opportunity to prove itself good, even at the risk of discovering that you’ve paid the artist an undeserved compliment.

What Lewis is calling us to hear is more than just giving the artist the benefit of the doubt. He’s asking us to risk mistaking bad art for good art. Why? Because the alternative is much worse. If you refuse to take that risk, you are far less likely to be surprised, delighted, and transformed.

Lewis said that if even one person could love for a lifetime something that you thought was bad, it should raise the suspicion that it can’t be as bad as you thought.

Good critics assume that there may be something there that they just can’t see yet. They are “easy to please but hard to satisfy”. They are generous with their attention but discerning with their judgment.1

1.In The Abolition of Man, Lewis argues that education involves learning to love and admire what deserves our admiration. Good taste is a matter of trained affections, not just personal preference.

The less attention we give a work, the more likely we are to substitute judgment for description. Modern criticism is full of this (e.g. cringe, mid). It’s what Lewis (in another book) called verbicide.

To judge fairly, you need to describe what the work does before deciding whether it does it well. Word count here is like rope. Give a shallow criticism enough of it, and it’ll hang itself.

Lewis believed that negative criticism should require more work (i.e. “a negative proposition is harder to establish than a positive”). It’s one thing to say “I hated this.” It’s another to say “This is terrible.” The first statement describes your experience. The second one makes a claim about the work itself. And failing to find anything good in a work doesn’t establish that nothing good is there.

Lewis asks us to judge posture before we judge taste. Most people reverse this. They think, This person likes x, so they must have bad taste. But why people like something is far more important and, frankly, far more interesting than what they like.

Lewis reminds us that good criticism begins with the willingness to be wrong. Or, more fundamentally, the willingness to be surprised and delighted.
