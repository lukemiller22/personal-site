Surprised by Joy — C. S. Lewis

Created May 28, 2013 · Updated November 25, 2026 · 592 words

Surprised by Joy is the spiritual autobiography of C. S. Lewis.

The pursuit of this elusive Joy is the theme of the entire book. Lewis defines it early on, after three childhood experiences of it:

Lewis chases the familiar feeling in the things that once stirred it, but those things only disappoint him. Then Joy surprises him again, when he least expects it, in some new truth or beauty.

Meanwhile, nearly everything he loves seems imaginary, and nearly everything he believes is real seems grim and meaningless. Books, art, philosophy, and friendship slowly wear down his atheism. Owen Barfield, for one, dismantles his chronological snobbery. In the end, he finds that Joy was never the thing he wanted. It only pointed beyond itself to God.

Selected quotes

Adversaria

The title comes from Wordsworth’s sonnet about the grief he carries for his daughter Catherine, who died in 1812 at age three. He experiences a sudden moment of joy, and he turns to share it with her. Then he remembers that she is gone.

The Gray Havens album Blue Flower was inspired by this book. The album title is taken from a passage in which Lewis describes the elusiveness of Joy: “And every day there were what we called ‘the Green Hills’; that is, the low line of the Castlereagh Hills which we saw from the nursery windows. They were not very far off but they were, to children, quite unattainable. They taught me longing—Sehnsucht; made me for good or ill, and before I was six years old, a votary of the Blue Flower.”
