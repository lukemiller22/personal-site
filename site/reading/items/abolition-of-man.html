The Abolition of Man — C. S. Lewis

Created January 31, 2017 · Updated November 18, 2026 · 450 words

In part 1, we learn that The Abolition of Man is a book about education and the subtlety of propaganda.

Lewis believes that education is capable of destroying children, and, through them, of destroying great civilizations.

In part 2, Lewis takes aim at subjectivism:

For Lewis, the recognition of objective value is the foundation of a proper education. If values are discovered rather than invented, then the task of education is to cultivate in children an appreciation of what is truly good.

In other words, when we are done with this kind of education, we will often be shocked at the success of our efforts.

In part 3, Lewis concludes with a rather depressing vision of man’s supposed “conquest” over nature. This conquest is really just radical rebellion. It is man’s attempt to take God’s role in the world He has made. If we take this path, Lewis predicts that we will make two startling discoveries:

  1. Man’s conquest of nature actually turns out to be some men’s conquest of other men. The idea of “nature” is just an excuse for this abuse of power.
  2. Nature’s conquest over man is the final play in this game, and it leaves mankind degraded and despised.

In the short appendix at the end of the book, Lewis provides a concise yet convincing collection of evidence in support of what he calls the Tao and what we would call “Natural Law” or “Traditional Morality” (in natural revelation, God reveals; in natural law, man discovers). He then goes on to show what monotonous agreement there has been on ethical issues throughout the history of the world.
